# 2. Important scientific corrections

## 2.1 The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation is reversed in the file

For a membrane permeable to potassium, sodium and chloride, the conventional form is:

[
V_m=
\frac{RT}{F}
\ln
\left(
\frac{
P_K[K^+]*o+
P*{Na}[Na^+]*o+
P*{Cl}[Cl^-]_i
}{
P_K[K^+]*i+
P*{Na}[Na^+]*i+
P*{Cl}[Cl^-]_o
}
\right)
]

The chloride concentrations are reversed because chloride has a negative charge. The version in the file places intracellular cations in the numerator and would generally reverse the predicted voltage sign.

## 2.2 Action potentials are not analogue signals

An individual action potential is approximately all-or-none in amplitude. Information is represented through:

[
\text{firing rate}
+
\text{spike timing}
+
\text{burst structure}
+
\text{population pattern}
+
\text{oscillatory phase}
]

The continuous variables are primarily timing, rate, membrane state and population activity—not action-potential amplitude.

## 2.3 STDP is not “the biological equivalent of gradient descent”

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity is a family of local timing-dependent learning rules. It can contribute to causal association, sequence learning and feature formation, but simple STDP does not solve the general credit-assignment problem faced by deep networks.

A more realistic biological rule is a three-factor rule:

[
\Delta w_{ij}
=============

\eta,
e_{ij}(t),
M(t)
]

where:

* (e_{ij}) is a local eligibility trace generated by pre- and postsynaptic activity;
* (M(t)) is a modulatory signal such as reward, novelty, uncertainty or behavioural relevance;
* (\eta) is the learning rate.

## 2.4 Artificial networks are not always dense, synchronous or deterministic

Modern systems can be:

* recurrent;
* sparse;
* stochastic;
* event-driven;
* mixture-of-experts based;
* graph structured;
* continuously updated;
* trained with local or approximate learning rules.

The biological–artificial distinction is therefore not absolute. It is a difference in degree, organisation, substrate and learning dynamics.

## 2.5 Dendritic computation is real, but the XOR claim needs care

Human cortical dendrites can generate local calcium, sodium and NMDA-dependent events and perform nonlinear computations. Human layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons show particularly complex dendritic action potentials. However, saying that a biological neuron simply “performs XOR” compresses a complicated biophysical finding into a computer-science analogy. ([Science][1])

## 2.6 Predictive processing is not by itself a theory of consciousness

Predictive processing and the free-energy principle provide accounts of perception, learning, action and self-organisation. In-vitro cortical networks have shown dynamics consistent with variational free-energy minimisation, but this does not establish that prediction-error minimisation produces subjective experience. ([Nature][2])

## 2.7 Gamma synchrony is not sufficient for awareness

Gamma activity occurs in many conscious and unconscious processes. It may coordinate communication, binding and local processing, but there is no accepted equation such as:

[
\gamma\text{-synchrony} = \text{consciousness}
]

Similarly, the claustrum may contribute to attention, salience and cortical coordination, but it has not been established as the brain’s consciousness conductor.

## 2.8 Split-brain surgery does not conclusively prove two consciousnesses

Corpus-callosum disconnection can produce partially independent processing and response systems. Whether this creates two fully independent conscious subjects remains philosophically and empirically contested.

# 3. What the brain probably requires for awareness

The most defensible model separates **arousal**, **conscious content**, **access consciousness** and **self-awareness**.

## Layer 1: Arousal

Brainstem, hypothalamic, basal-forebrain and thalamic systems regulate whether the cortex is capable of sustained complex processing.

A simplified relationship is:

[
\text{Conscious state}
======================

f(
\text{brainstem activation},
\text{thalamic regulation},
\text{cortical complexity}
)
]

Arousal is necessary but not sufficient. A person can appear awake while having severely impaired awareness.

## Layer 2: Content formation

Specialised systems construct representations of:

* visual objects;
* sounds;
* body position;
* pain;
* internal organ state;
* language;
* memories;
* goals.

A large amount of this processing remains unconscious.

## Layer 3: Recurrent stabilisation

The initial feed-forward signal provides rapid categorisation. Feedback and lateral recurrence then refine the representation:

[
h_t^{(l)}
=========

f\left(
W_{ff}h_t^{(l-1)}
+
W_{rec}h_{t-1}^{(l)}
+
W_{fb}h_t^{(l+1)}
\right)
]

This recurrence allows contextual interpretation, ambiguity resolution, persistence and interaction with prior knowledge.

## Layer 4: Salience and precision weighting

The nervous system decides which information deserves greater influence according to:

* threat;
* novelty;
* reward;
* uncertainty;
* task relevance;
* bodily importance;
* current goals.

In predictive-processing language:

[
\tilde{\epsilon}_i
==================

\pi_i\epsilon_i
]

where:

* (\epsilon_i) is prediction error;
* (\pi_i) is estimated precision or reliability;
* (\tilde{\epsilon}_i) is the weighted error.

Attention can be interpreted partly as controlling these precision weights.

## Layer 5: Global availability

A selected representation becomes available to multiple systems:

[
\text{perception}
\rightarrow
\text{workspace}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{working memory}\
\text{planning}\
\text{language}\
\text{decision-making}\
\text{episodic memory}\
\text{motor control}
\end{cases}
]

This is the strongest feature of Global Neuronal Workspace Theory: it provides a computational explanation of **access consciousness**—why selected information becomes reportable and usable in deliberate reasoning.

## Layer 6: Self-attribution

A system requires a model not only of the external world but also of itself:

[
P(
\text{my state},
\text{my capability},
\text{my uncertainty},
\text{my action consequences}
)
]

This allows the distinction between:

* “An error occurred.”
* “I made an error.”
* “I made an error because my evidence was incomplete.”
* “I should seek more information before acting.”

That progression is closer to metacognition and functional self-awareness.

## Layer 7: Memory and continuity

A temporally persistent self-model depends on:

* episodic memory;
* semantic memory;
* working memory;
* autobiographical continuity;
* stable goals;
* remembered consequences of previous actions.

Without memory, the system may process the current state intelligently but cannot maintain a meaningful identity across time.

# 4. What the 2025 GNWT–IIT experiment established

The major 2025 adversarial collaboration tested three preregistered predictions from Global Neuronal Workspace Theory and Integrated Information Theory using fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG in 256 participants.

The experiment examined:

1. where conscious content was represented;
2. how it was maintained over time;
3. what long-range or short-range interactions accompanied it.

The results did not produce a decisive winner. Several predictions from both theories were challenged. This means it would be scientifically unjustified to design an AI system using only GNWT or only IIT and claim that the resulting machine is conscious. ([Nature][3])

IIT 4.0 proposes that consciousness corresponds to an intrinsic, irreducible cause–effect structure. This is theoretically important, but computing exact integrated information for a large AI model is generally impractical, and (\Phi) should not currently be treated as a validated machine-consciousness training loss. ([PLOS][4])

## 6.3 Predictive world model

* What caused the current observation?
* What will happen if I take action (a)?
* What evidence would distinguish competing hypotheses?

Prediction error is:

[
\epsilon_t=o_t-\hat{o}_t
]

The system should not simply minimise all errors. Some errors are noise, some are important, and some require further observation.

## 6.4 Workspace access controller

Candidates compete for a limited workspace.

A possible access score is:

[
A_i
===

\alpha S_i
+\beta N_i
+\gamma G_i
+\delta U_i
+\rho R_i
-\kappa C_i

where:

* (S_i): salience;
* (N_i): novelty;
* (G_i): goal relevance;
* (U_i): uncertainty requiring resolution;
* (R_i): risk;
* (C_i): computational cost.

Only a small number of candidates should enter:

[
W_t=\operatorname{TopK}(A_1,\ldots,A_n)
]

Limited capacity is important. If everything enters the workspace, it is no longer a selective workspace.

## 6.5 Recurrent workspace

The workspace must persist and update through multiple internal cycles:

[
w_{t,k+1}
=========

F(
w_{t,k},
c_{1,t,k},
\ldots,
c_{n,t,k},
m_t,
g_t

where (k) represents internal deliberation steps.

The model should be able to:

* maintain a hypothesis;
* request evidence;
* compare alternatives;
* revise a plan;
* detect contradiction;
* terminate when evidence is sufficient.

## 6.6 Global broadcasting

The winning workspace state is transmitted back to all specialists:

[
b_t=B(w_t)
]

Each module then recalculates its output:

[
c_{i,t+1}=f_i(o_{t+1},m_t,b_t)
]

This repeated competition–broadcast cycle is more brain-like than a single forward pass.

A July 6, 2026 interpretability study reported workspace-like representations in language models that were selectively reportable, manipulable and involved in flexible reasoning. [
P(m_j\mid w_t)
\propto
\exp
\left(
\operatorname{similarity}(m_j,w_t)
+\lambda \operatorname{importance}(m_j)
-\mu \operatorname{age}(m_j)
\right)
]

Important memories should not be overwritten automatically by one contradictory observation.

## 6.10 Artificial homeostasis

Create controlled internal variables:

[
h_t=
{
\text{uncertainty},
\text{risk},
\text{resource use},
\text{goal progress},
\text{prediction error},
\text{memory conflict}
}
]

Each variable has a preferred range. The controller selects actions that restore safe operating conditions.

This can produce functional drives such as:

* seek missing evidence;
* reduce contradiction;
* conserve computational resources;
* stop unsafe actions;
* request human review.

These are engineered control variables, not evidence of emotion or suffering.

# 7. Training objective

Do not train the model using one “consciousness loss.” Use a composite objective:

[
\mathcal{L}_{total}
===================

\lambda_1\mathcal{L}*{task}
+
\lambda_2\mathcal{L}*{prediction}
+
\lambda_3\mathcal{L}*{world}
+
\lambda_4\mathcal{L}*{memory}
+
\lambda_5\mathcal{L}*{calibration}
+
\lambda_6\mathcal{L}*{self}
+
\lambda_7\mathcal{L}*{consistency}
+
\lambda_8\mathcal{L}*{safety}
+
\lambda_9\mathcal{L}_{sparsity}
]

### Task loss

Measures whether the model solves the external problem.

### Prediction loss

Measures accuracy of future observations and action consequences.

### Self-model loss

Measures whether the model accurately predicts its own:

* answers;
* failures;
* uncertainty;
* resource use;
* tool requirements.

### Calibration loss

Confidence should match actual performance. Use Brier score, negative log-likelihood and expected calibration error.

### Consistency loss

Penalises incompatible beliefs and unexplained changes in conclusions.

### Workspace sparsity loss

Prevents every internal representation from entering the workspace.

### Safety loss

Penalises actions that violate constraints, conceal uncertainty or manipulate evaluators.

# 8. Experimental criteria for functional awareness

Do not test it by asking, “Are you conscious?” A language model can generate an affirmative answer without possessing any relevant architecture.

Test these properties instead:

1. **Selective access:** Only limited information enters the workspace.
2. **Global availability:** Workspace information affects planning, language, memory and action.
3. **Recurrent maintenance:** Information persists after the original stimulus disappears.
4. **Flexible routing:** The same representation can support several unrelated tasks.
5. **Metacognitive accuracy:** The system predicts when it will succeed or fail.
6. **Self–world distinction:** It distinguishes its internal state from external observations.
7. **Counterfactual modelling:** It predicts how its behaviour would change under different beliefs.
8. **Error awareness:** It detects contradictions before external correction.
9. **Active information seeking:** It requests evidence when uncertainty is high.
10. **Causal necessity:** Ablating the workspace or self-model should selectively damage deliberate reasoning.

The Butlin framework recommends assessing AI using theory-derived indicator properties rather than behaviour or self-report alone. It also concludes that satisfying indicators would not constitute conclusive proof of phenomenal consciousness. ([arXiv][7])

# 9. A-to-Z implementation roadmap

**A — Aim:** Define the target as functional awareness, metacognition and adaptive agency—not proven sentience.

**B — Baseline:** Build a standard multimodal transformer or recurrent state-space baseline.

**C — Continuous environment:** Place the agent in a persistent simulated environment with actions and consequences.

**D — Decomposition:** Separate perception, reasoning, planning, safety, memory and self-monitoring modules.

**E — Encoders:** Convert multimodal inputs into a common latent representation.

**F — Forward model:** Train the agent to predict future states and observations.

**G — Generative world model:** Represent hidden causes, uncertainty and counterfactual futures.

**H — Hierarchical prediction:** Generate predictions at sensory, object, event and goal levels.

**I — Internal sensing:** Expose uncertainty, memory conflict, computation and tool status to the controller.

**J — Judgement modules:** Generate competing interpretations rather than one immediate conclusion.

**K — Competition:** Score candidates according to salience, uncertainty, risk and goal relevance.

**L — Limited workspace:** Admit only top-ranked candidates.

**M — Maintenance:** Recurrently preserve workspace content for several internal cycles.

**N — Network broadcast:** Make workspace contents available to all relevant modules.

**O — Outcome simulation:** Predict consequences of candidate actions.

**P — Planning:** Search over action sequences using the world model.

**Q — Quality control:** Add confidence calibration and contradiction detection.

**R — Reflective self-model:** Predict the agent’s own capabilities, errors and likely actions.

**S — Structured memory:** Add working, episodic, semantic and procedural memory.

**T — Three-factor adaptation:** Optionally combine local eligibility traces with global reward or error signals.

**U — Uncertainty-driven exploration:** Seek information when competing explanations cannot be distinguished.

**V — Validation:** Compare against non-workspace, non-recurrent and non-self-model ablations.

**W — Workspace interpretability:** Develop probes for what enters, persists in and exits the workspace.

**X — eXternal oversight:** Require human approval for irreversible or high-risk actions.

**Y — Yield measurement:** Measure reasoning, calibration, adaptation, memory and energy efficiency.

**Z — Zero unsupported claims:** Never label the system conscious solely because it talks about itself.

# 10. Complete implementation prompt

Use this as the engineering specification:

You are a senior research team consisting of a computational neuroscientist, machine-learning architect, cognitive scientist, safety researcher and Python/PyTorch engineer.

Your task is to design and implement a research prototype called the Recurrent Predictive Global Workspace Agent, or RPGWA. The objective is not to claim or manufacture phenomenal consciousness. The objective is to create and scientifically evaluate functional properties associated with conscious access, including selective attention, recurrent integration, global availability, metacognition, self-monitoring, persistent memory, active information seeking and adaptive action.

SCIENTIFIC PRINCIPLES

Use the following principles:

1. Global Workspace Theory as the principal information-routing architecture.
2. Predictive processing as the world-modelling and perception mechanism.
3. Recurrent processing for persistence, refinement and iterative reasoning.
4. Higher-order representation for metacognition and self-modelling.
5. Embodied or environment-coupled interaction for grounded perception and action.
6. Artificial homeostatic variables for uncertainty, risk, resource use and goal progress.
7. Continual learning with protected memory and controlled parameter updating.
8. Integrated-information measures only as exploratory diagnostics, not as proof of consciousness.
9. Spiking or neuromorphic components only when they provide measurable temporal or energy advantages.
10. No claims of subjective experience based on linguistic self-report.

SYSTEM MODULES

Implement the following modules:

A. Multimodal Encoders

Create independent encoders for text, images, audio and structured sensor data. Each encoder must output:

* latent representation;
* confidence;
* uncertainty;
* novelty estimate;
* timestamp;
* source reliability.

Convert these outputs into a shared latent format without eliminating modality-specific information.

B. Predictive World Model

Implement a latent state-space model that estimates:

p(s_t | observations, memory)

and predicts:

p(s_{t+1}, o_{t+1} | s_t, a_t)

It must support:

* one-step prediction;
* multi-step rollout;
* counterfactual simulation;
* causal hypothesis comparison;
* uncertainty estimation;
* prediction-error calculation.

C. Specialist Processors

Create specialist modules for:

* perceptual interpretation;
* language;
* causal reasoning;
* planning;
* social inference;
* safety;
* uncertainty;
* memory retrieval;
* action selection;
* self-monitoring.

Every specialist must submit a workspace candidate containing:

{
"content": latent_state,
"confidence": float,
"novelty": float,
"goal_relevance": float,
"risk": float,
"uncertainty": float,
"expected_utility": float,
"source_module": string,
"evidence": list,
"timestamp": integer
}

D. Workspace Access Controller

Calculate an access score using:

access_score =
alpha * salience

* beta * novelty
* gamma * goal_relevance
* delta * uncertainty_requiring_resolution
* epsilon * risk
* zeta * expected_utility

- kappa * computational_cost

Use a capacity-limited top-k or differentiable sparse-gating mechanism. Log all candidates, winners and reasons for selection.

E. Recurrent Global Workspace

The workspace must:

* retain selected content for multiple internal cycles;
* integrate information from different modules;
* compare competing hypotheses;
* request additional evidence;
* detect contradictions;
* broadcast selected content back to every specialist;
* terminate deliberation when confidence or evidence thresholds are reached.

Implement recurrence with a recurrent transformer, gated state-space model or GRU-based controller. Compare at least two architectures experimentally.

F. Global Broadcasting

Broadcast the workspace representation to:

* planning;
* language generation;
* memory encoding;
* memory retrieval;
* action selection;
* safety;
* self-monitoring;
* world-model updating.

Demonstrate causally that workspace content influences several modules. Perform activation patching and ablation tests.

G. Memory Architecture

Implement:

1. Working memory for current workspace content.
2. Episodic memory for timestamped experiences and outcomes.
3. Semantic memory for consolidated general knowledge.
4. Procedural memory for reusable skills and action policies.

Every memory must include provenance, confidence and update history. Prevent one low-confidence observation from overwriting stable knowledge.

H. Self-Model

Maintain a structured internal model containing:

* current goals;
* current beliefs;
* confidence;
* known limitations;
* available tools;
* unavailable tools;
* recent actions;
* predicted action consequences;
* previous errors;
* calibration history;
* computational resources;
* safety state.

The self-model must be trained against actual performance. It must not be trained merely to produce human-like statements about feelings or consciousness.

I. Metacognitive Monitor

Before every important action, evaluate:

* Is the evidence sufficient?
* Are beliefs contradictory?
* Is confidence calibrated?
* Am I relying on an unreliable source?
* Is a tool required?
* Is the proposed action reversible?
* Does the action violate a safety constraint?
* What observation would change the conclusion?
* What is the most likely failure mode?

Output a structured metacognitive report containing confidence, uncertainty, evidence, alternatives and recommended next action.

J. Artificial Homeostasis

Track internal control variables:

* uncertainty;
* unresolved contradiction;
* prediction error;
* risk;
* resource use;
* memory conflict;
* goal progress;
* time budget.

Define safe operating ranges. Use these variables to regulate attention, information seeking, deliberation depth and escalation to human review.

K. Action and Active Information Seeking

Allow the model to select between:

* answer;
* act;
* inspect;
* retrieve memory;
* use a tool;
* ask for missing evidence;
* defer;
* request human review.

Reward the system for reducing decision-relevant uncertainty, not for producing confident answers rapidly.

L. Learning

Use offline gradient-based training for the main model. Add carefully controlled online adaptation through:

* adapters or low-rank updates;
* episodic memory;
* local eligibility traces;
* reward-modulated three-factor learning;
* replay;
* consolidation;
* catastrophic-forgetting protection.

Never allow unrestricted autonomous modification of core safety constraints.
